About P-Code

Supporting screening for Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) — formerly known as PCOS — with explainable AI you can understand.

Understanding PMOS (formerly PCOS)

P-Code uses the name PMOS because the international clinical community is moving away from PCOS to a label that better matches how the condition actually works.

Polyendocrine

Hormone systems beyond a single pathway can be involved — including androgens and reproductive hormones.

Metabolic

Insulin resistance, weight, and long-term cardiometabolic risk are often central — not side notes.

Ovarian

Cycles and ovarian function matter, but the condition is not defined by pathological “cyst disease.”

Why PCOS was renamed

  • The old name was inaccurate. “Polycystic” suggested abnormal ovarian cysts are the core problem. Evidence shows that is a poor description for many people with the condition.
  • It hid the bigger picture. Focusing on ovaries overshadowed endocrine and metabolic features that affect daily life and long-term health.
  • It contributed to delays and stigma. Misunderstanding the name has been linked to delayed diagnosis, fragmented care, and less serious treatment of the condition as a multi-system syndrome.

How it became PMOS

A multi-year global consensus process — involving patient organizations, clinicians, and professional societies — used surveys, workshops, and structured decision methods to choose a clearer name. The preferred consensus name is Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS).

Implementation is designed as a transition (education + guideline alignment), with the new name expected to be fully reflected in the international guideline update around 2028. During the transition you may still see “PCOS” in older materials; P-Code uses PMOS and notes that it was formerly known as PCOS.

Early awareness & screening literacy

Home shows a short early-detection guide once you are signed in. Here is the fuller context behind those overview cards — what PMOS can look like, why timing matters, which inputs improve screening quality, and how to use results responsibly.

What PMOS can look like

Presentation varies widely. Overlap with other conditions is common, so symptoms alone never equal a diagnosis.

  • Cycle changes — irregular, missed, very long, or unpredictable periods; ovulatory disruption for some people.
  • Skin & hair — acne, hirsutism, or thinning scalp hair related to androgen excess for some.
  • Weight & metabolism — weight change, central adiposity, or insulin-resistance risk markers.
  • Mood & energy — fatigue, anxiety, or low mood reported by some people living with the condition.
  • Fertility concerns — difficulty conceiving or needing fertility workups for some.

Why early detection matters

  • Earlier support — recognizing patterns sooner can lead to earlier symptom management and quality-of-life help.
  • Metabolic follow-up — insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk are easier to discuss when they are on the table early.
  • Cycle & fertility planning — clearer conversations about options before crises or delayed care.
  • Less “cyst myth” delay — accurate framing (PMOS, not “dangerous cysts only”) reduces false reassurance or dismissal.

P-Code supports awareness and preparation for clinical visits. It does not diagnose or treat PMOS.

Inputs that improve screening quality

You can screen with partial information. Completeness usually makes the clinical and overall summaries clearer.

  • Symptoms & vitals — cycle history, blood pressure, height/weight (BMI), waist and hip for WHR when available.
  • Hormones (when you have labs) — LH, FSH, and AMH are commonly used to contextualize ovulation and ovarian reserve patterns; other labs your clinician orders may also matter.
  • Ultrasound (optional) — morphology cues (e.g., follicle pattern) can support the imaging model when an image is available; absence of ultrasound does not block clinical screening.
  • Re-screening — update saved inputs when labs or images change so History and XAI reflect your latest information.

Supportive habits (general wellness)

These are general wellness ideas — not treatment plans and not a substitute for clinician advice.

  • Movement — sustainable activity most people can maintain week to week.
  • Nutrition — balanced meals; limiting frequent sugary drinks where possible.
  • Sleep — more consistent sleep timing when your schedule allows.
  • Stress — small daily routines that reduce stress load.

On Home (signed in), Recommended next steps and Improve screening quality stay personalized from your latest screening — those live on the dashboard, not here.

How P-Code works in depth

The Home dashboard keeps a short “How to use P-Code” overview. This section explains what each product surface actually does, what the models output, and where explainability and limitations apply — without repeating the model-spec cards below.

Community screening journey

Detect — dual-input screening

On Detect you enter structured clinical information (symptoms, measurements, optional hormone values) and may attach an ultrasound image. The system produces three related summaries:

  • Imaging (CNN) — when an ultrasound is present; otherwise may show pending.
  • Clinical (XGBoost) — from your structured inputs.
  • Overall — a combined screening view with probability-style confidence, intended for discussion with a clinician.

Results, History, and your data

After you save, Home can show your latest screening cards. History keeps a timeline of past screenings so you can compare visits over time. “Your data” surfaces what you stored so you can update incomplete labs or images later — completeness usually improves clinical and overall clarity, not “guaranteed accuracy.”

Nearby care & hard boundaries

“Find care near you” uses Google Maps Places to list OB-GYN / women’s health / hospital / ultrasound-related facilities near your location (specialty noise like dental or eye clinics is filtered). Listings are reference-only — P-Code does not verify, endorse, or book providers. The product never claims to replace professional diagnosis or care. Explanations for why a result looked a certain way live on Explainable AI below.

Clinical / OB-GYN decision support

Patient workflow support

The provider portal is built for structured intake, imaging attachment, and screening review alongside clinical judgment. Outputs are decision-support summaries (probabilities, pending states, explainability hooks) — not a Rotterdam (or other guideline) diagnosis by themselves. Clinicians remain responsible for history, exam, labs, imaging interpretation, and care plans.

How to read dual-model outputs

Treat imaging and clinical arms as complementary signals. Discordance (e.g., strong clinical signal with pending/weak imaging) is expected when ultrasound is missing or low quality — investigate data completeness before over-interpreting the overall score. Confidence displays are model-derived screening aids, not calibrated diagnostic certainty.

Performance transparency

Where available, model-performance views surface precision, recall, F1, and related validation summaries so teams can see strengths and failure modes before relying on the tool in a workflow. Metrics describe historical evaluation — they are not a promise of accuracy for any individual patient.

Advanced AI Technology Stack

Model-level detail for the two engines behind Detect. Product workflow is covered above; SHAP and EigenCAM are covered in Explainable AI below.

XGBoost

eXtreme Gradient Boosting

XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) serves as our primary engine for structured clinical data analysis. It excels at handling the complex, non-linear relationships between hormonal markers, metabolic indicators, and PMOS diagnosis.

CNN

Convolutional Neural Network

Our CNN (Convolutional Neural Network) specializes in automated ultrasound image interpretation, identifying key morphological features such as follicle count, distribution patterns, and ovarian volume that are diagnostic of PMOS according to Rotterdam criteria.

Explainable AI

P-Code does not stop at a score. Explanations show what the models paid attention to so community users can prepare better clinic questions, and clinicians can review model focus alongside judgment — without treating explanations as a diagnosis or a formal radiology report.

SHAP (clinical model)

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) attributes how much each structured input — for example cycle patterns, hormone-related values, or basic measurements — pushed the XGBoost clinical result up or down for that screening.

Use it to see which saved fields mattered most. It supports follow-up conversations; it does not replace medical judgment or lab interpretation.

EigenCAM (imaging model)

When an ultrasound is available, EigenCAM highlights image regions the CNN attended to more for the imaging part of the screening — a visual hint around structures such as follicles or ovarian borders.

It helps connect an imaging result to a picture. A clinician or imaging specialist still provides the official read.
